Decision Report  ·  Corporate Fleet Procurement

Which vehicles
to buy, and why

A plain-language answer to the question:
“How do I spend $1.55 million on a delivery fleet
and get the most capability out of it?”
The short version

We can buy you 63 vehicles for $1,548,641 with a combined 7,146 horsepower that meets your 30 mpg sustainability target — but only if you allow eleven of them to fall four horsepower below your towing threshold.

Hold that threshold firm and the 30 mpg target becomes impossible. Not expensive — impossible. No budget fixes it.

01What you asked us for

You gave us six rules. Here they are as we understood them, in your own words, alongside what we did with each one.

What you saidHow we handled it

“Every vehicle needs at least 100 hp for towing” Used as a filter. Vehicles under 100 hp were removed from consideration entirely — which turned out to be the pivotal decision in this whole report.
“At least 60 vehicles” Hard floor on fleet size.

“Prefer European manufacturers if you can” Noted as a preference, not a rule. We could not enforce it strictly (see section 5), but our recommendation happens to include twelve European vehicles.

What we searched

The vehicle catalogue contains 398 listings covering 301 distinct models. After removing listings with missing engine data and everything below your 100 hp towing threshold, 132 models remained eligible. We then examined every possible combination of those 132 models — every mix, every quantity — and identified the single best one. This is not a sample or an estimate. It is the provably best answer available from this catalogue under your rules.

03The one rule we could not meet

Your 30 mpg fleet average cannot be achieved. We want to be precise about why, because the reason is not the one people usually assume.

Once you require every vehicle to produce at least 100 horsepower, the most fuel-efficient vehicle left in the entire catalogue manages 32.9 mpg. There are exactly a handful of models near that mark, and you are capped at twelve units of each. Build the most economical fleet those constraints permit and you land at 29.82 mpg.

You are short by 0.18 mpg. Less than one percent. And there is nothing you can do about it.

Why relaxing a rule makes the fleet stronger

This is counter-intuitive, so it is worth a moment. The 30 mpg target applies to the fleet average, not to each vehicle. The eleven Toyotas are extremely economical at 32 mpg, which creates headroom in the average — and that headroom lets you buy genuinely powerful vehicles elsewhere, including a 225 hp Buick that your original rules could never have afforded to accommodate. Eleven slightly weaker vehicles buy you the freedom to make the other fifty-two considerably stronger.

The decision in front of you

Everything hinges on one question: is the 100 hp towing threshold a hard engineering requirement, or a round number somebody picked?

If it is a real limit — a trailer weight, a legal classification, a safety certification — take Option A and tell corporate the 30 mpg target is unattainable at any budget. If it is a rule of thumb, take Option B. You get more capability, you satisfy sustainability, and you get the European vehicles you wanted, for twelve thousand dollars more.

05What we assumed

Every number in this report rests on the assumptions below. If any of them is wrong, tell us and we will re-run the analysis in under a minute.

Prices are estimated, not quoted

This is the assumption most worth your attention. The catalogue contains no prices. It lists engine power, weight, fuel economy, model year and origin — but not what anything costs. Rather than abandon the analysis, we estimated each vehicle's price from its curb weight at $9.40 per pound, which puts a typical vehicle in this catalogue at roughly $26,000.

The recommendations hold as long as heavier vehicles genuinely cost more than lighter ones, which is broadly true. But the exact dollar figures are estimates. Give us a real price list and the answer may shift — the method will not.

Everything else

  • “About $1.55M” was read as a firm ceiling of exactly $1,550,000. If you can flex to $1.6M, say so; it does not change the fuel-economy finding, but it may change the mix.
  • The 30 mpg target was applied to the fleet average, not to each vehicle individually. Applied per-vehicle it would be far more restrictive, and Option B would not exist. Please confirm which you meant.
  • Where the same model appeared several times in the catalogue at different weights, we used the cheapest listing.
  • Six listings had no engine-power figure recorded and were excluded rather than guessed at.
  • Fuel economy figures are the catalogue's own, unadjusted for load, terrain or duty cycle. A working delivery fleet will do worse in practice — the comparison between options remains valid, but treat 30 mpg as a catalogue figure rather than a promise.

What we could not answer

You asked us to prefer European manufacturers. We could not enforce this properly. The catalogue records only a broad region — USA, Japan or Europe — with no manufacturer detail beneath it, and no way to distinguish, say, a German manufacturer from an Italian one. We therefore treated it as a tie-breaker rather than a rule. Option B includes twelve European vehicles as a consequence of the numbers, not because we steered toward them. If European sourcing matters to you commercially, we need a supplier list to do it justice.

How this analysis was produced. An AI agent read the raw vehicle catalogue, worked out for itself which columns represented cost, power and efficiency, translated your written instructions into a formal mathematical model, and handed that model to an optimisation engine that searched the full space of possible fleets. When your original rules turned out to be unsatisfiable, the agent identified precisely which rule was responsible and by how much it would need to bend, rather than silently discarding it. Every figure in this report was produced by that engine and can be reproduced exactly. Nothing here was estimated by eye.
